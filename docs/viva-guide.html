Your 30-second answer

“Chest X-rays often show several diseases at once, and some diseases are very rare. I fine-tuned a DenseNet-121 network on about 112,000 NIH chest X-rays to give 14 separate probabilities, one per disease. I handled the rare diseases with a weighted loss and a separate decision threshold for each disease.

A good score doesn’t prove the model looks at the disease, so I added Grad-CAM heatmaps and measured how often they land inside boxes drawn by radiologists, compared with a random baseline. A Gradio web demo shows the predictions and heatmaps for any uploaded X-ray.”

Learn this one by heart. Every question below expands one sentence of it.

Big picture

What happens to one X-ray

The project has two paths through the same network. The top path makes the prediction. The bottom path, Grad-CAM, reuses what the network computed to show where the evidence for one disease came from.

Training

How the model learns

A convolutional neural network (CNN) is a stack of small learned filters that slide across the image. Early layers respond to edges, middle layers to textures and shapes, and deep layers to larger patterns such as the outline of the heart or fluid at a lung base. Nobody programs these filters. They start as numbers and are adjusted, millions of times, until the predictions match the labels.

Transfer learning: the network does not start from random numbers. It starts from weights already trained on ImageNet (about 1.3 million everyday photos), so it already knows edges and shapes. All layers are then fine-tuned on X-rays. This trains faster and works better than starting from scratch.

Loss
One number that says how wrong the predictions are. Training tries to make it smaller.
Backpropagation
Works out, for every weight, whether nudging it up or down would lower the loss (the gradient).
Optimizer (AdamW)
Takes a small step on every weight in the helpful direction. Step size is the learning rate (1e-4, shrinking on a cosine schedule).
Overfitting
Memorising the training images instead of learning general patterns. Detected by watching a separate validation set; handled with early stopping, augmentation and pretrained weights.
Mixed precision
Doing most GPU maths in 16-bit numbers: faster, less memory, almost no accuracy loss.
Problem 1

Why 14 separate answers

A normal image classifier picks one answer, using softmax: all outputs share a total of 1.0. That is wrong here, because one X-ray can show effusion and cardiomegaly. Your model uses a sigmoid on each output instead, so each disease gets its own probability from 0 to 1.

Problem 2

Rare diseases and the accuracy trap

Some diseases are common, some are very rare. Hernia appears in about 1 image in 500. A lazy model that always answers “no Hernia” is 99.8% accurate and completely useless. That is why accuracy is never used as the metric in this project.

The project fights imbalance in two places: during training (the loss) and when making the final yes/no decision (the thresholds). The three loss options are compared as experiments.

loss: weighted_bce · main model

Weighted cross-entropy

Missing a positive case costs more for rare diseases. The weight is negatives ÷ positives, so a missed Hernia costs several hundred times more than usual.

loss: focal

Focal loss

Shrinks the loss on easy examples the model already gets right (mostly the many healthy images), so training concentrates on hard ones.

loss: asl

Asymmetric loss

Like focal loss, but it is stricter with negatives than positives and ignores very easy negatives entirely. Built for multi-label data.

evaluate.py · tune_thresholds

Per-disease thresholds

Instead of saying “yes” above 0.5 for everything, each disease gets the cut-off that gives the best F1 score on the validation set.

Honest results

Fair testing: split by patient

Many NIH patients have several X-rays taken on different days. If one scan of a patient is in training and another scan of the same patient is in testing, the model can partly recognise the person instead of the disease. Scores then look better than they really are. This is called data leakage.

Train (~78k images)
What the model learns from.
Validation (~8.6k)
10% of training patients, held out. Used to pick the best epoch and to tune the thresholds.
Test (25,596)
The official NIH test list. Touched once, at the end, for the numbers in your report.
Augmentation
Small random changes to training images (rotation up to 7°, 5% shift, 5% zoom, 15% brightness and contrast) so the model copes with real-world variation. No horizontal flip, because the heart sits on the left and a mirrored chest is not a realistic patient.

Measuring results

For each disease separately, every test image falls into one of four boxes:

Sensitivity (recall)
Of the patients who really have it, how many did we catch? TP ÷ (TP + FN)
Specificity
Of the healthy patients, how many did we correctly clear? TN ÷ (TN + FP)
Precision
Of the patients we flagged, how many really had it? TP ÷ (TP + FP)
F1
One number balancing precision and recall (their harmonic mean). Used to choose each threshold.
AUROC
Pick one sick and one healthy X-ray at random. AUROC is the chance the model gives the sick one the higher score. 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect. It needs no threshold, which is why papers on this dataset use it as the main metric.
AUPRC
Area under the precision–recall curve. More honest for rare diseases, because it ignores the huge pile of easy true negatives. Its random baseline is the disease’s prevalence (0.002 for Hernia), not 0.5.
Your results. Mean test AUROC 0.80 for all three losses (0.801 plain BCE, 0.799 weighted, 0.798 focal), which is a statistical tie. Diseases with clear visual signs scored highest (hernia 0.90, emphysema 0.90, cardiomegaly 0.89). Vague ones with noisy labels scored lowest (infiltration 0.69, pneumonia 0.70). DenseNet-121 models at 224 px usually reach about 0.80–0.84 on this dataset. The full per-disease table is in the readme and in metrics_test.json.
Problem 3 · Explainability

How Grad-CAM makes a heatmap

The last convolution layer outputs a 7×7 grid with 1024 channels. Each channel is a detector for some learned pattern, and its 7×7 values say where in the image that pattern was found. Grad-CAM asks which channels mattered for one particular disease, then adds up their maps.

  1. Run the X-ray through the network and keep the 7×7×1024 feature map.
  2. Pick one disease, say Effusion, and compute the gradient of its score with respect to the feature map: how much the score would change if each value changed.
  3. Average the gradient over each channel’s 7×7 grid. That average is the channel’s importance weight for Effusion.
  4. Multiply each channel by its weight, add them all up, and keep only positive values (ReLU). This is the 7×7 heatmap.
  5. Scale it from 0 to 1, enlarge it to the image size, and colour it over the X-ray.
weight_k = mean over 7×7 of ∂ score_disease / ∂ A_k heatmap = ReLU( Σ_k weight_k × A_k ) # A_k = channel k of the feature map
One map per disease
Each disease has its own score, so each gets its own heatmap. Effusion should light up a lung base; cardiomegaly should light up the heart.
Grad-CAM++
A refinement that weights each grid position individually instead of using a plain average. It often covers several separate regions better, for example effusion on both sides. Your project reports both.
Why it is blurry
Each of the 7×7 cells covers about 32×32 pixels of the 224-pixel input. The heatmap shows a region, not an exact outline, so tiny nodules localise poorly.
Your original contribution

The box check: is the model cheating?

Shortcut learning means the model finds something that happens to go with a disease in the training data, but is not the disease. Known examples on chest X-rays: pneumothorax models that partly detect the chest drain inserted to treat it (Oakden-Rayner et al., 2020); models that recognise which hospital an image came from (Zech et al., 2018); COVID-19 models that relied on text labels and image borders (DeGrave et al., 2021).

NIH released 984 boxes drawn by radiologists around real lesions, for 8 diseases. Your localization.py computes each box’s disease heatmap and grades it against the box.

pointing_hitShare of boxes where the heatmap’s hottest point is inside the box (the “pointing game”).Well above box_fraction
energy_in_boxShare of the heatmap’s total heat that falls inside the box.Well above box_fraction

box_fractionBox area ÷ image area: the score a random heatmap would get.This is the baseline, not a result

The red flag to look for: a disease with a high AUROC whose energy_in_box is close to box_fraction. The model is good at the label but is not getting its evidence from the lesion. Open the worst_*.png images for that disease and look for tubes, wires, text markers, shoulders or image edges.
